# Quit India, 1942, and the Secret Congress Radio: research dossier

Prepared for the game design team. Every quote below has been checked against the source named next to it. Confidence labels: **solid** (primary record, or two or more independent sources agree), **disputed** (sources conflict; the range is given), **reported** (one secondary source only; usable with care), **unverified** (repeated widely, but no record found).

Spelling convention: the record spells several names more than one way. All of these appear in primary documents: Khakhar / Khakar / Khakkar; Motwane / Motwani; Printer's middle name Adarbad / Abarbad / Abrabad. The game should pick one spelling and keep it. Forgery traps should use a *wrong person or place*, not one of these real spelling variants.

---

## 1. Summary

On the night of Saturday 8 August 1942 the All-India Congress Committee (AICC), meeting in a pandal at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay, passed the "Quit India" resolution. Only 13 members voted against. Gandhi then gave the "Do or Die" mantra. At 5:00 a.m. the next morning, Sunday 9 August, about sixty police parties arrested the whole Congress leadership in Bombay at once. The British called this moment "zero hour". A special train had the leaders out of the city by 7:15 a.m. That same morning Aruna Asaf Ali raised the Congress flag at Gowalia Maidan while the police put on gas masks and threw tear gas. With the leaders in jail and the press gagged by an order of 8 August, India went through what the Viceroy privately called "by far the most serious rebellion since that of 1857". Inside it, a 22-year-old Wilson College student named Usha Mehta and a handful of young Gujarati friends ran a clandestine shortwave station. It opened with "This is the Congress Radio calling on 42.34 metres from somewhere in India." The team moved the transmitter between at least six Bombay buildings in under three months, recorded programmes on home-cut gramophone discs in a separate studio, and broadcast speeches written by Ram Manohar Lohia. CID stenographers took down every broadcast from 8 October. The station was finally betrayed by its own paid technician, Nariman Printer, after police leaned on radio-shop staff. On Thursday 12 November 1942 police broke through three doors while the "Vande Mataram" record was ending. Exactly what happened in that room was later argued in court. For a game about checking real messages against forgeries, the story is unusually rich. It has a real station with a signature tune, real police transcripts, real false names and fake company bills, a real informer, real Axis broadcasts that listeners mistook for it, and a real trial that turned on whether a gramophone needle was resting on a record.

## 3. Persona options

**A. The announcer-operator.** A composite of the Usha Mehta and Chandrakant Jhaveri role at the Parekh Wadi transmitter. Each day a courier brings a script or a cut disc from the recording station, plus slips of "news" from all over India. The player must decide what goes on air before 8:45 p.m. The fit with the game is near-perfect: the real station really did receive news "by special messengers" (Usha Mehta 1969), Sucheta Kripalani's office "used to prepare the daily bulletins thst were read over this radio" (Kripalani oral history), and the station really did hold back unconfirmed reports. On 26 October it told listeners it had sat on a story about soldiers shot at Meerut "as it was not co[nfirmed]". The forger is plausible: CID plants, Axis propaganda, and a technician who is already talking. Losing condition: broadcast a fake and you either discredit Congress, the thing the British white paper set out to prove, or you reveal your location.

**B. The courier between the recording station and the transmitter.** Usha Mehta describes "a band of volunteers who used to help us carry the records from the recording station to the transmitting station." K. A. Abbas says the discs "could be played, I think, eight or ten times." Sucheta Kripalani describes couriers "from the richest houses, dressed in a most fashionable way, who travelled first class." This persona carries suitcases through monsoon Bombay (Girgaum, Walkeshwar, Mahalaxmi), checks each instruction slip against what they know about addresses, code names and times, and meets fake contacts. It suits a map or route layer.

**C. The CID Special Branch monitor or censor (antagonist view).** A stenographer under "Inspector Fergusson" and Ganesh Keshav Kokje, logging transcripts from 8 October ("Voice indistinct"; "station closed abruptly at 9:40 pm"), checking intercepted letters ("Congress know well our power of interception", Hallett, May 1942) and enforcing the 8 August press order. The player sorts genuine underground traffic from planted or Axis material. This is morally uncomfortable for an Indian audience. If used, make it a second campaign, or frame it like the real officials who leaked to the underground: Achyut Patwardhan said a member of the official committee hunting the underground "regularly informed him" (Bipan Chandra et al.).

The recommendation is A as the main mode, with B as a variant.

## 5. Real messages (seeds for "genuine" telegrams)

**Congress Radio broadcasts** (Bombay police transcripts, published in Sengupta and Chatterjee 1988, quoted in Chatterjee 1989 and Huacuja Alonso 2023, and in Usha Mehta's 1969 interview):
- "This is Congress Radio calling from 42.34. Now we will give you news in Hindi. The entire world is aware that a ban has been laid on the publication of news nowadays." (20 Oct)
- "We offer Id greetings to our Mohamedan brothers… History tells us that lakhs and crores of Muslims of this country have always fought for the freedom of this country shoulder to shoulder alongwith us." (12 Oct)
- "We are always on the air from 8:45 pm till you hear Vande Mataram. Move between 39 and 43 meters, which at times is necessary." (18 Oct)
- "Revolution for freedom is the revolution for the poor. Free India will be of the workers and peasants." (27 Oct)
- "Remember whenever you touch any part of the machine in the factory you dip your hands in the blood of your countrymen and you strengthen the slavery of your motherland." (27 Oct)
- To railwaymen: "You are helping that government by driving the railway, which has killed 75 thousand of your brothers." (October; the figure is the station's claim)
- "Radio is the only place where you can find uncensored news." (30 Oct)
- "A month ago we received news that about 306 soldiers were shot in Meerut by usurper government but we did not give publicity to this news as it was not co[nfirmed]…" (26 Oct; the claim itself is unverified, but this is a real example of the station checking news before airing it)
- "Let the whole country resound with a million voices all trained to talk uniformly by the only voice of truth and freedom in the country, the Congress Radio calling." / "Never talk of the persons behind the stations." (morning, 12 Nov)
- Lohia, on air: "So far we were conducting movements but now we are conducting a revolution. In a revolution there is victory or defeat. This revolution is not of one party or community but of the whole nation." (Usha Mehta 1969)
- "India has no enmity for the British or any other people." (Usha Mehta 1969)
- Gandhi Jayanti, 2 Oct: "Celebrate the day with great enthusiasm and revolutionary fervour and with a bang." (Usha Mehta 1969)
- 19 Oct, 9:07 p.m., English, on soldiers' rapes at Chimur: "Without hesitation we answer: do all that you can… Rape is outside politics." (Sensitive. Do not use as a gameplay prop.)

## 6. Real deception: betrayals, tricks and codes

**Inside the station**
- **The paid technician.** Printer joined "purely for financial motivations" (court). The judge found he "knowingly deceived his colleagues about having increased the strength of the station's transmitter". After his arrest he led police in and cut the fuse. He received immunity.
- **The front man.** R. A. Mehta "procured and rented the flats… using a false name", then cooperated with police and was not tried (Huacuja Alonso).
- **The fake firm.** The microphone bill was made out to "Messrs Babubhai & Co, Laxmi Building, Fort". The judge disbelieved Killewala's story that he had paid for it himself.
- **Tradecraft.** Equipment travelled in suitcases while the team posed as "a family moving apartments". Usha Mehta stopped wearing khadi saris to avoid notice. Recording and transmitting stations were kept apart, and discs were carried between them. On air: "somewhere in India" and "Never talk of the persons behind the stations." Code names: "Doctor", "Kusum" (a man's code name that sounds like a woman's), "Bahanji".
- **A legal loophole.** V. K. Jhaveri's handwriting was on the seized gramophone records, but he was acquitted because records were held not to be "documents".

**The police hunt**
- Monitoring by stenographers from 8 Oct; "detecting van" chases ("very often it was merely a question of touch and go", Usha Mehta). Kokje watched radio-shop sales and questioned a shop attendant, which led him to Printer. Radio dealers were raided a week before the arrests, and technicians, including Jagannath Thakur, talked. K. A. Abbas says "Central C.I.D. specialists" came from Delhi.
- **A sting (reported).** Police let a second transmitter be built at Tanna Radio Acoustics and arrested the buyers who came to collect it on 31 Oct.
- **Jamming.** "The official radio used to jam our broadcasts", and the team tried "jam[ming] the broadcasts of the official radio" back (Usha Mehta 1969). This is her claim; no British confirmation was found.

**Intercepted mail and planted papers**
- UP Governor Hallett, 10 May 1942: "We have recently intercepted the original draft of the 'war' resolution… Congress know well our power of interception and usually send important documents by special messenger." He added a theory that the draft was posted deliberately "so that it might be intercepted" (TOP II pp. 63–4). The government later published these "Allahabad documents" against Congress (TOP II).
- TOP II also mentions an intercepted letter "from Miss Pandit", and the Sindh Governor writes "I have seen (intercepted by the C.I.D.) the Maulana's reply to Allah Bakhsh" (TOP II, pp. 46–7).

**False news on all sides**
- **Axis look-alikes.** CPI members told Abbas the Congress Radio "is Nazi Radio which is operating from Germany, Italy or probably from Japan and they just called themselves Congress Radio" (Abbas, CCAS 123b). A. C. N. Nambiar reportedly testified that Bose had planned a secret "Congress Radio" and an "Azad Muslim Radio" that would appear to broadcast from inside India. This is reported via Nathan Morley's *Radio Hitler* (2021); whether such a station actually aired in 1942 is **unverified**.
- **British suspicion and its outcome.** The British first suspected fifth-column backing. H. V. R. Iyengar, who listened to Tokyo and Saigon, testified the broadcasts "do not furnish any specific evidence of fifth column inspiration". The judge agreed.
- **The white paper's selection.** The government's 1943 report reprinted a "Congress Bulletin No. 5" containing pro-Japanese lines and false stories that Indian regiments in Egypt and North Africa had been shot for refusing to fight. Who wrote that bulletin cannot be checked. Treat it as a period example of disinformation, whoever produced it.
- **The press fights back.** Fifteen Calcutta papers suspended publication from 21 to 31 Aug in protest (IAR). One day editorials appeared as "blank white columns" (Kripalani).

**Underground disguises**
- Sucheta Kripalani wore fine khadi "which looked like mill cloth", kept silk saris for emergencies, and before Bombay changed into "a very loud-coloured… Madrasi silk saree" so a CID watcher looking for a woman in white missed her. She asked a fellow passenger to post a card to her sister-in-law if she was arrested.
- Underground leaders spent a night "as patients" in Dr Baliga's clinic.
- J. N. Sahni was met at an emptied hideout by men who said: "We are C.I.D. men… we are also in search of it, but it is gone from here!" (CCAS 203b)

**After the raid**
- The Bombay Congress Bulletin of 6–9 Jan 1943 promised the radio would be back Tue and Thu at 9 a.m. and Sun at noon. Police heard only "a short announcement… on Saturday on a wavelength of 6.55 MC" (police report via Huacuja Alonso n.85).

## 7. Tempting wrong variants (DESIGN INPUT, not facts)

- **People:** Vithalbhai *Jhaveri* (real) vs Vithalbhai *Patel* (died 1933). Nariman *Printer* vs K. F. *Nariman* (a Bombay Congress leader). Sucheta Kripalani (underground) vs Acharya J. B. Kripalani (in jail). Aruna Asaf Ali (raised the flag) vs Sarojini Naidu (arrested at dawn). Achyut Patwardhan vs Vitthal Rao Patwardhan (Nasik). Usha Mehta vs Usha *Thakkar* (her historian, an anachronism). Babubhai Khakhar vs "Babubhai Desai". Rao Bahadur *Desai* (Special Branch) vs "Mahadev Desai" (Gandhi's secretary, also arrested).
- **Places:** Gowalia Tank Maidan vs Azad Maidan, Shivaji Park or Chowpatty sands. Walkeshwar Road vs Warden Road. Laburnum Road vs Lamington Road. Sandhurst Road vs Hughes Road. Girgaum *Back* Road vs Girgaum Road. Mahalaxmi Temple vs Mumbadevi Temple. Parekh Wadi vs Khetwadi. Sea View vs "Sea Face". Aga Khan Palace (Gandhi) vs Yerwada Jail (others) vs Ahmednagar Fort (Working Committee). Ahmednagar vs Ahmedabad. Chinchwad vs Kalyan.
- **Numbers and units:** 42.34 vs 43.24, 41.87 or 24.34. "Metres" is right; "MHz" and "FM" are anachronisms (period usage was "Mc" or "megacycles", and shortwave AM). 8:45 p.m. vs 8:15 or 9:45. 13 votes against vs 31. Rs 599-8-0 vs "Rs 599.50" (decimal currency is an anachronism). Raid on Thursday 12 Nov vs "Friday". War time GMT+6:30 vs a message that says IST is "5½ hours ahead".
- **Anachronisms that give a forger away:** a tricolour with the Ashoka Chakra (the 1942 Congress flag had the charkha); "Jana Gana Mana, the national anthem" (adopted 1950); "Ae Mere Watan Ke Logon" (1963); "Netaji's INA marching on India" in 1942 (Bose reached East Asia in 1943); "Mumbai", "Kolkata", "Akashvani" (adopted officially later); tape recorders (the station used home-cut discs, which Abbas notes explicitly).
- **British habits vs underground habits:** British papers say "Mr. Gandhi", "the disturbances", "rebellion", "Moslem", "Poona", "zero hour", "His Excellency", and "Repeated to…". The underground says "Gandhiji", "Mahatmaji" or "Bapu", "August Revolution", "Inquilab Zindabad", "Azad Hind", and "Anti-India Radio" for AIR. A "Congress" broadcast that calls him "Mr. Gandhi" or names its own street is suspect, because the station never gave its location. A "police" memo that calls him "Bapu" is suspect too.
- **Role swaps:** Lohia "reading Hindi news" (he relayed English); "Usha Mehta, Elphinstone College student" (she was at Wilson College); "Motwane of Calcutta Radio" (it was Chicago Radio).

## 8. Myths vs facts

1. **"Operation Zero Hour."** British papers do use "zero hour", but as a generic term for the planned strike moment ("Zero hour was fixed at 5-00 a.m."; Hallett's "zero hour should be immediately after meeting"). No document read for this dossier shows a code-named "Operation Zero Hour". Use "zero hour" without "Operation".
2. **"Leaders arrested on the night of 8 August."** In Bombay the arrests came from 5:00 a.m. on 9 Aug. Some arrests elsewhere, for example in Bengal according to Mookerjee, began on the evening of 8 Aug.
3. **"The radio began on 14 August."** That is Usha Mehta's 1969 memory. The police and court record says 27 Aug, and the Congress bulletin announced the station on 3 Sep. Present it as disputed.
4. **"Only 42.34 metres."** The court record says 41.78 m for the start. Both are real.
5. **"Police burst in while Vande Mataram was playing and Usha refused to stop it."** That is her account ("We did not oblige them"). The judge held that Printer had cut the fuse before the police entered and that the photographs showed the needle off the record (Huacuja Alonso 2023). Tell it as "she said / the court found".
6. **"Usha Mehta got 5 years."** She got 4; Babubhai Khakhar got 5. Wikipedia's Congress Radio article has this wrong.
7. **"She was tortured."** Usha Mehta wrote her own account partly to correct accounts that "incorrectly claimed that the police had tortured her" (NMML document via Huacuja Alonso).
8. **"Heard across India and in Burma."** It was a low-power set, and reception was poor even in Bombay. Bipan Chandra et al. say it was heard "as far as Madras". Claims of Burma reception are unverified.
9. **"Usha Mehta ran it alone."** Police named Khakhar the chief organiser. Lohia funded and wrote for it, Printer built it, and Vithalbhai ran recording. Hers was the main voice, and the judge's "lieutenant" label understated her role.
10. **"Caught by direction-finding vans."** The vans chased the station, but it was taken through radio-shop surveillance and informers (technicians, then Printer).
11. **"Ae Watan Mere Watan (2024) shows what happened."** It is fiction.
12. **"The official toll was 10,000."** The official figure was 1,028 killed. Nehru estimated "Perhaps 10,000 may be nearer the mark", and noted popular estimates of 25,000. These are an estimate and a rumour, not official counts.
13. **"Mookerjee was deputy CM in a Muslim League ministry in Bengal."** He was Finance Minister in Fazlul Huq's Progressive Coalition, and the League was in opposition (*Leaves from a Diary*, introduction). Hindu or Mahasabha ministers sitting in League-led ministries are documented or reported for Sindh (Hidayatullah, from Oct 1942) and NWFP (1943). See §12.
14. **"Quit India was launched on 9 August."** The resolution passed on 8 Aug; 9 Aug, now August Kranti Day, marks the arrests and the uprising.
15. **"Congress Radio at 7.12 MHz from 2 September"** (Wikipedia and amateur-radio retellings). This is not in the court record. 7.12 MHz would be about 42.1 m.

## 9. Sensory world

- **Sound:** shortwave hiss that fades in and out; a whistle when tuning between 39 and 43 m; the hum of valves warming up; a gramophone crackle before "Sare Jahan Se Achcha" and then "Vande Mataram". The court record notes the AICC used Master Krishnarao's rendition, probably the one the station played. Also a voice the police log marks "indistinct"; Hindustani news, then English; a pause as the operator swaps home-cut discs ("eight or ten" plays each); three doors forced in turn; the click of a fuse and sudden dark. In the streets: monsoon rain (August–September), tram bells, lathi blows and police whistles, a crowd shouting "Inquilab Zindabad", and "Karenge ya marenge". At Gowalia Maidan on 9 Aug: a European sergeant's order, the hiss of tear-gas canisters, a captain shouting "All lie down", and coughing.
- **Objects on the operator's desk:** a homebuilt valve transmitter (crystal-controlled according to one account); a Philips "check" receiver for monitoring; a microphone (the Rs 250 one); an amplifier and pick-up; stacks of hand-labelled discs (police seized about 120); bedrolls; a kerosene lamp (used after the fuse blew); typed bulletins from Sucheta Kripalani's office; cyclostyled "Congress Bulletins"; cash memos from Chicago Radio in annas and rupees; a suitcase.
- **Clothes:** khadi for open Congress workers; fine khadi that "looked like mill cloth", or mill saris, for those underground; a loud silk sari as disguise; Desh Sevikas in "attractive orange robes" in ordered rows at Gowalia Maidan; plain-clothes CID men on staircases; police gas masks.
- **Light and place:** top-floor Bombay flats (Sea View, Laxmi Bhuvan, Parekh Wadi on the "top" or fifth floor); Chowpatty sea air; Malabar Hill and Walkeshwar bungalows; Girgaum chawls; humid nights; blackout-era wartime Bombay; a pandal lit for 10,000 at Gowalia Tank.
- **Print:** blank editorial columns; walls pasted with bulletins; leaflets ending "make and distribute ten copies of this".

## 10. Sensitivity notes and the range of views

- **Congress/Gandhian view:** a non-violent mass struggle forced into the open by arrests before Gandhi could negotiate ("That process is likely to take two or three weeks", his 8 Aug speech). In jail Gandhi refused to condemn the violence and blamed the state's "leonine violence" (Bipan Chandra et al. p. 470). Bipan Chandra et al. argue that spontaneity and prior organisation were both present.
- **Socialist view (Lohia, JP, Patwardhan, Aruna Asaf Ali):** the underground leadership saw its role as keeping morale up and keeping a line of command, and it accepted sabotage of communications. Even Gandhian associates said cutting wires was permissible if life was protected (GoI 1943 report, App. XIV).
- **British view:** the government's case was that Congress organised the "rebellion" (Maxwell: "we cannot absolve the Congress from responsibility"; the 1943 white paper). In private, Linlithgow admitted the gravity of the situation and the failure of intelligence.
- **The Communist Party, Muslim League, Hindu Mahasabha, RSS and others:** see §12, with their own words and their own defences.
- **Non-violence and violence:** the record includes crowd killings (two RAF officers at Fatwa; Nehru cites official reports of "about 100 persons" killed by mobs) and very heavy state violence: firing on 538 occasions, collective fines of Rs 90 lakh, and soldiers' rapes at Chimur reported by the radio and by women investigators. Show both plainly.
- **Axis confusion:** Bose's Axis-backed radio supported the uprising. Congress Radio never voiced support for the Axis (judge's finding). Keep that distinction, because both sides of Indian politics care about it.
- **Communal framing:** the station opened every night with Iqbal's song and sent Id greetings. Muslim participants included Moinuddin Harris on air, Bombay's mayor Yusuf Meherally (arrested), and Sindh Premier Allah Bux, who gave up his British titles. Criticise organisations' positions, never communities.
- **Romanticisation:** Usha Mehta herself pushed back on exaggerated accounts. The "she said / court found" approach works well and is accurate.

## 12. Who sat out or worked against Quit India, with receipts

Ground rule: this section sets out what organisations and office-holders did in 1942, in their own words, together with each one's own defence. Members of every group below joined the uprising as individuals, against their leadership's line. Bipan Chandra et al. note "hundreds of Communists at the local and village levels participated". None of this is about a religion or community. For balance: a Muslim Premier gave up his British titles in protest, a Muslim speaker was one of the radio's voices, and Hindu Mahasabha leaders publicly demanded talks with Gandhi.

**1. Communist Party of India**
- *Stance:* the CPI treated the war as a "People's War" after the Nazi attack on the USSR (June 1941). It was legalised in July 1942. Its AICC members voted against the resolution; they were the 13. Its Sept 1942 Central Committee resolution set up "vigilance committees" in mills to "check… irresponsible provocateurs intent on sabotage or stoppage of production."
- *Receipt:* CPI open letter to the Working Committee, July 1942: "Such a 'struggle' to-day will not liquidate but strengthen the bureaucracy." Also: "Is it not plain enough that to start your 'struggle' is just to play the game of the imperialists and the bureaucrats?" (reprinted in Joshi 1945, App. I, pp. 291–3; marxists.org open-letter.pdf). CC resolution, Sept 1942: the struggle is "the suicidal path of sabotage" (solve-national-crisis.pdf).
- *Their defence:* the same Sept resolution "condemns unequivocally the military and police rule of blood and terror" and demands "Release Mahatma Gandhi and the National leaders." P. C. Joshi's cable of Aug 1942 said: "Our fellow patriots have been provoked to their present suicidal course by the imperialist rulers" (IAR 1942 II, Aug chronicle). In 1945 Joshi wrote: "In the Bombay AICC, however, no one dared to say that ours was an anti-national stand", and quoted Gandhi congratulating "the thirteen friends who voted against" (Joshi 1945, pp. 104–5).

**2. All-India Muslim League**
- *Receipt:* Jinnah, 9 Aug 1942: "I deeply regret that the Congress has finally declared war and has launched a most dangerous mass movement" (IAR 1942 II, Chronicle p. 17). League Working Committee, Bombay, 20 Aug 1942: it "call[s] upon the Muslims to abstain from any participation in the movement initiated by the Congress and to continue to pursue their normal peaceful life" (TOP II No. 598, p. 774). In September Jinnah "congratulated the Muslims for completely keeping themselves aloof" (IAR, Sept chronicle).
- *Their defence:* "The Muslims are not a whit less insistent on freedom for the country", but the movement aimed at "the establishment of Hindu raj and to deal a death blow to the Muslim goal of Pakistan" (TOP II pp. 772–3). Jinnah, 7 Aug: "Muslim India… stands for complete independence of all the peoples of India", but an immediate National Government "would have meant Hindu Raj" (IAR p. 17). The same resolution also rebuked the British for being "unresponsive to the Muslim League offer of co-operation."

**3. Hindu Mahasabha**
- *Receipt, Savarkar, early Sept 1942:* "I issue this definite instruction to all Hindu Sabhaites in particular and all Hindu Sanghatanists in general who happen to be members of the municipalities and local bodies, legislatures, or Committees or those who may be serving in the army, navy, air force or working in ammunition factories or holding any post or position of vantage in Government service that they should stick to the posts and continue to perform their regular duties" (IAR 1942 II, Sept chronicle).
- *Receipt, Syama Prasad Mookerjee, then Bengal Finance Minister, letter to Governor Sir John Herbert, 26 July 1942:* "The question is how to combat this movement in Bengal? The administration of the province should be carried on in such a manner that in spite of the best efforts of the Congress, this movement will fail to take root in the province." Also: "Anybody, who during the war, plans to stir up mass feelings… must be resisted by any Government that may function for the time being." (*Leaves from a Diary*, OUP 1993, pp. 179, 183)
- *Coalitions:* Bengal (Dec 1941 – Mar 1943) was Fazlul Huq's Progressive Coalition with Mookerjee in it, and the League in opposition. In Sindh, Hidayatullah's League-led ministry was "completed… with the appointment of two Hindus, namely, Dr. Hemandas Wadhwani and Rai Saheb Gokaldas Mewaldas" (IAR, Oct 1942). Wadhwani is usually described as a Mahasabha leader, but the 1942 entry does not say so. Savarkar on 10 June 1943: "boycotting the Ministry altogether is bound to be more often than not highly detrimental to Hindu interest"; Mahasabhaites "should try to capture as many constitutional posts of vantage as possible" (*Historic Statements*, 1967). A Mahasabha minister in the NWFP League ministry (Mehr Chand Khanna, 1943) is reported but was not checked here.
- *Their defence:* Savarkar called the policy "responsive co-operation", which "implies all and any means from unconditional co-operation to an armed resistance", and argued that militarising Hindus served Hindu interests (statement of 8 Apr 1942, *Historic Statements*). Mookerjee wrote in the same letter that "mere repression is no remedy" and that "It is only by a transfer of power to Indians that you can hope to win the active and willing support of the people of Bengal" (pp. 179, 184). On 12 Aug he told the Viceroy that the Congress demand "virtually constitutes the national demand of India as a whole." He resigned in November, citing Midnapore repression and the Governor's interference (intro, pp. xi–xvi). Mookerjee, Moonje and N. C. Chatterjee "demanded an immediate consultation with Mahatma Gandhi", and the Bengal Mahasabha protested "the policy of repression" on 11 Aug (IAR).

**4. Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh**
- *Receipt:* M. S. Golwalkar, later: "In 1942 also there was a strong sentiment in the hearts of many. At that time, too the routine work of Sangh continued. Sangh vowed not to do anything directly." (*Shri Guruji Samagra Darshan*, vol. IV, p. 40, Hindi. English as translated by Shamsul Islam; original not seen.) A Bombay Government report, as quoted by Andersen and Damle, *The Brotherhood in Saffron* (1987), p. 44: "the Sangh has scrupulously kept itself within the law, and in particular, has refrained from taking part in the disturbances that broke out in August 1942" (archival file not seen).
- *Their defence:* the RSS-aligned Organiser says swayamsevaks took part as individuals (Chimur) and that Delhi RSS leader Lala Hansraj Gupta sheltered Aruna Asaf Ali (organiser.org, 2024). Historians quoted by The Wire find no evidence of organised participation. Golwalkar's own line, in the same passage, says many volunteers were "greatly disgusted" by the inaction.

**5. Princely states**
- *Receipt:* a Viceroy's note of 25 Aug 1942 says the Indian States Forces had grown "from 46,600 to 79,000 men", about 100,000 state subjects had enlisted, and war donations were "Rs. 3,12,80,000 non-recurring". It adds that "Nearly all the Rulers' Palaces in Delhi are being used as offices" (TOP II pp. 817–8). Several states suppressed local Quit India activity (*Rebel India* on Bhopal, Rajgarh and others).
- *The other side:* Maharaja Holkar of Indore, 25 Aug: "The isolationism of Indian States is now a thing of the past… [they should] associate themselves more directly with national aspirations" (IAR). Aundh sheltered Satara's Prati Sarkar activists (Bipan Chandra et al. p. 472).

**6. Title-holders (Rao/Rai/Khan Bahadur and others)**
- *Receipt:* the Special Branch officer "most intimately concerned" with the arrests "was a Gujarati officer, Rao Bahadur Desai", praised by Governor Lumley (TOP II p. 807).
- *Counter-receipt:* Khan Bahadur Allah Bux, Premier of Sindh, "renounced his titles of 'Khan Bahadur' and 'O.B.E.' as a protest", saying: "It is the cumulative result of the feeling that the British Government does not want to part with power. Mr. Churchill's speech shattered all hopes." He was dismissed on 10 Oct 1942 (IAR). The record does not support treating title-holders as one bloc.

**7. B. R. Ambedkar (Labour Member, Viceroy's Executive Council, from July 1942)**
- *Receipt:* Governor Lumley: "Dr. Ambedkar responded to your suggestion by a very downright denunciation of Congress on behalf of the Depressed Classes" (TOP II p. 805). His biographer reports Ambedkar called the movement "both irresponsible and insane, a bankruptcy of statesmanship", and said it would be madness to weaken law and order "at a time when the barbarians were at the gates" (Keer 1954, p. 354, partly paraphrased).
- *His stated reasons and nuance:* the Japanese threat, and his long argument that Congress did not represent the Untouchables (*Mr. Gandhi and the Emancipation of the Untouchables*, 1943). Inside the Council he argued that "if we wanted peace in this country it meant giving the Congress its own terms" (Linlithgow to Amery, 17 Aug 1942, TOP II No. 577, p. 744).

**8. Others**
- **C. Rajagopalachari:** had broken with the Congress line in 1942 over the Pakistan question. He issued a press statement "calling for a stoppage of all activities against the Government" (*Rebel India*, Madras section).
- **National Liberal Federation:** "condemned the disturbances… particularly when the enemy is knocking at the doors of India" (IAR, Sept 1942).
- **Indian members of the Executive Council:** M. S. Aney, N. R. Sarkar and H. P. Mody "supported the Government in its suppression of the 1942 movement", then resigned in Feb 1943 rather than "be a party to Gandhiji's death" during his fast (Bipan Chandra et al. p. 470).
