# Delhi 1857: Meerut to the fall of Delhi (May to September 1857)

Research dossier for the game. Source keys in [brackets] are listed in section 11. Confidence labels: **solid** = two independent primary sources, or one primary plus a good scholarly source. **Disputed** = sources disagree, and the range is given. **Single-source** = only one source found.

---

## 1. Summary

On the evening of Sunday 10 May 1857, troopers of the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry and the 11th and 20th Bengal Native Infantry at Meerut rose in revolt. They freed 85 comrades who had been shackled the day before for refusing cartridges, and rode through the night to Delhi. On Monday 11 May they crossed the Jumna bridge of boats, killed the British officials at the palace, and put themselves under the 81-year-old Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar. Delhi's own regiments (38th, 54th and 74th Native Infantry) joined them, the British blew up their own Magazine, and two young signallers in the telegraph office sent the warning to Ambala that let the Punjab disarm its sepoys. For four months Delhi was the political centre of the uprising. The city had a reluctant king, a quarrelling army that kept growing as regiments arrived from across north India, an improvised "Court" of soldiers and civilians, and newspapers printing hope and rumour. Spies ran both ways. A British force of a few thousand, later about 9,000, held the Ridge north of the city. The British stormed the city from 14 September, Zafar surrendered at Humayun's Tomb, and William Hodson shot three Mughal princes on the road. For the game, this is a city full of letters, petitions, intercepted notes, planted forgeries, spy reports written to please their readers, and rumours of Persian armies. A message-checker in Delhi in 1857 would have had real work to do.

## 2. Timeline

### Recommended window: 10 to 25 May 1857 (opening days)

Weekdays are computed from the Gregorian calendar and match the sources. The Hijri date comes from Jiwan Lal's diary, where 24 May is "the 29th of Ramzan" [JL p.102]. That makes 11 May roughly 16 Ramzan 1273 AH. In the Vikram Samvat calendar the year was 1914.

| **Sat 9 May** | At Meerut the 85 troopers of the 3rd Light Cavalry are publicly shackled on parade [USI; Kaye II]. |
| **Sun 10 May** | Delhi and Meerut telegraph offices close as usual on Sunday from 9 am to 4 pm. Before closing, the Meerut signaller chats that "eighty men had been imprisoned and were to be blown away from guns" (an exaggeration) [Luke p.253]. At 4 pm Delhi finds the Meerut line dead. Brendish and Pilkington cross the bridge of boats to test the Jumna cable and find the break lies beyond, towards Meerut [Luke pp.255-56]. In the evening Meerut rises and Col. John Finnis of the 11th NI is shot [USI]. At midnight the Meerut postmaster gets a private telegram through to his aunt at Agra: "Cavalry have risen setting fire to houses... If aunt intends starting tomorrow please detain her" [USI]. |
| **Mon 11 May** | Around 8 am Todd leaves in a pony gharry to repair the line and is killed near the Cable House on the left bank of the Jumna [Luke p.257; memorial in Fanshawe p.331]. Between 8 and 9 am rumours reach the city [JL p.75]. The troopers burn the toll-collector's bungalow and enter by the Raj Ghat gate [JL pp.78-79]. Commissioner Simon Fraser, Capt. Douglas, the chaplain Jennings, Miss Jennings and Miss Clifford are killed at and near the palace's Lahore Gate [JL pp.80-81]. Theophilus Metcalfe flees half-dressed to the Paharganj police station and police officer Mainodin hides him [MH pp.43-46]. Kaye says Ambala received the first message at 3 pm and the second at 4 pm [Kaye II pp.137-38]. In mid-afternoon (Mainodin says about 4 pm) Lt. At night citizens count a 21-gun salute and at first take it for a British victory [JL p.82]. |
| **Tue 12 May** | The Delhi regiments move into the city. Native officers present nazars (ceremonial gifts) to the King. Zafar rides through the streets on an elephant ordering shops to open. Letters go out to Patiala, Jhajjar, Ballabhgarh and other chiefs [JL p.85]. At Simla, Capt. Barnard delivers the telegram text to General Anson [Kaye II p.137]. |
| **Wed 13 May** | About 30 Europeans and Eurasians hiding in the Kishangarh Raja's house are given away by a water-carrier and killed [JL pp.87-89]. |
| **Thu 14 May** | Zafar shuts himself in. Messengers reporting a British force from Meerut are jailed as "spies sent by the English" [JL p.91]. |
| **Fri 15 May** | A 100-man city police force is formed. Sepoys accuse Ahsanullah and Mahbub Ali Khan of writing to the English. Mainodin passes a message to Metcalfe, now code-named "Shere Khan" [JL pp.91-92; MH p.57]. |
| **Sat 16 May** | Sepoys produce a seized letter "cursing the mutineers" and threaten to take Queen Zinat Mahal hostage. **49 to 52 European and Eurasian prisoners, mostly women and children, are killed** under a pipal tree by the small reservoir in the palace courtyard [JL pp.93-94; Trial c. pp.91-98]. |

| **Tue 19 May** | Gold mohurs struck in Zafar's name are presented. The jihad standard is raised at the Jama Masjid, and the King is angry [JL pp.96-98]. |
| **Wed 20 May** | Zafar to a deputation of Hindu officers: "The Holy War is against the English; I have forbidden it against the Hindus." Scouts sent out return "stark naked", stripped by Gujars [JL p.98]. |

Natural epilogue for this window: on 26 and 27 May guns on the Islamgarh bastion are found "filled with stones, gravel, and ends of string" (sabotage) [JL pp.103-04]. On 27 May Anson dies of cholera at Karnal [Kaye II; Rotton p.37]. On 28 May the troops quarrel over pay. On 29 May a "spy" disguised as a Hindu fortune-teller is denounced as John Lawrence [JL pp.104-07].

### Alternative window B: 28 July to 10 August (the "inside job" fortnight)
- 28 July: Zafar bans cow-killing in the city during Eid on pain of being blown from a gun [JL p.170].
- 1 August: Eid al-Adha. The King sacrifices a sheep at the Idgah, and a heavy attack on the Ridge follows [JL pp.176-77; Sen p.93].
- 2 August: Zafar tells his officers: "The Royal Treasury is empty and without a 'pice'" [JL p.178].
- 7 August: the powder factory in Begum Samru's house explodes. Jiwan Lal gives "four hundred and ninety-four persons" killed [JL p.186]. Ahsanullah is hunted, hidden behind the throne, then handed over. Jiwan Lal is arrested and accused: "This is the man who sends news to the English" [JL p.188].
- 7 and 14 August: Nicholson arrives, then his column (Sen p.98).

### Alternative window C: 7 to 22 September (the end)
- 3 or 4 September: the siege-train arrives. Batteries go up at Ludlow Castle, Qudsia Bagh and the old Custom House. On 10 September alone 39 unarmed Indian pioneers are killed or wounded building them [Sen p.104].
- 11 September: the breaching guns open fire.
- 13 September: an order goes out to the Kotwal (head of city police) under the commander-in-chief's seal [Trial c. p.131].
- 14 September (Monday): the assault. The Kashmiri Gate is blown open, and Nicholson is mortally wounded (he dies on 23 September) [Sen pp.105-07; MH p.69].
- 16 September: the Magazine is retaken.
- 20 September (Sunday): the Palace and Salimgarh are taken.
- 21 September (Monday): Zafar surrenders at Humayun's Tomb. Macdowell's letter says the 20th [Hodson pp.342-48; Sen p.110].
- 22 September (Tuesday): Mirza Mughal, Mirza Khizr Sultan and Mirza Abu Bakr are shot by Hodson near the Delhi Gate. Macdowell says the 21st [Hodson pp.340-41].

### Wider arc in brief
- 8 June: Battle of Badli-ki-Serai. The rebels lose 26 guns and the British occupy the Ridge [Sen p.79].
- 9 June: the Guides arrive after marching 580 miles from Mardan.
- 23 June: the centenary of Plassey and a fierce attack.
- 1 and 2 July: Bakht Khan arrives with the Bareilly brigade and is made Commander-in-Chief.
- 5 July: Barnard dies. Reed takes command until 17 July, then Archdale Wilson.
- 25 August: Nicholson defeats the Nimach brigade at Najafgarh.
- 27 January to 9 March 1858: Zafar's trial in the Diwan-i-Khas.
- 7 November 1862: Zafar dies in exile at Rangoon.

## 3. Persona options

**A. The signaller, Delhi Telegraph Office (11 May only).** A short, tense opening level. It is grounded in P.V. Luke's account, which he took from Brendish himself [Luke pp.245-67].
- **The job:** messages flow both ways on a needle instrument. Ambala tests unknown senders with the real query "What is your name?" [Luke p.265].
- **What the player knows:** the office closes 9 to 4 on Sundays; the Meerut line is dead; Todd went out at 8 am.
- **The forger:** imitates Delhi chatter with small errors, such as "Mr. Tod", "4th Cavalry", or a Morse-sounder habit.
- **Strength and weakness:** the stakes are huge and the material is verified. It only covers about 8 hours.

**B. A munshi at the Red Fort darbar reading arzis (petitions) and perwanahs (orders), May to September.** Modelled on real figures such as Mukund Lal, the King's secretary, and on Jiwan Lal's daily court diary. The Mutiny Papers are thousands of petitions and orders (Farooqui's blurb says over 10,000 documents).
- **The job:** each day brings a petition, a soldier's demand, a spy report, a newspaper item, or a letter to a chief. The player stamps it genuine or forged.
- **Real planted forgeries exist:** a letter forged in the names of Mahbub Ali and Ahsanullah to the Lieutenant-Governor at Agra [MH p.59], and a petition from Raja Gulab Singh that was "believed to have been forged" [Ahsanullah's statement in Majumdar p.126].
- **Strength:** the best fit for the game's loop, with the richest real texts and a natural day-by-day diary structure.

**C. A cossid (secret courier) between the city and the Ridge, July to September.** He carries letters written on thin "India paper", sewn into a shoe sole, hidden in a hollow stick, or stitched inside fake tape fastening a jacket's buttons [KY pp.243-44].
- **The job:** the case file covers routes, gates, codes and who is really loyal. Real reports were "coloured in a high degree to please us" (Gen. Norman's note) [KY p.173]. This adds a lovely twist: a genuine message can still be biased.
- **Optional variant:** the spy-handler's desk, i.e. Rajab Ali's tent under Hodson. It inverts the roles: the player is the British intelligence clerk sorting real from false informers. Use with care, given the tone brief in section 12.

## 4. Case-file facts (the true reference set)

1. **The Meerut regiments** were the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry and the 11th and 20th Bengal Native Infantry. The British troops there were the 60th Rifles (1st Battalion), the 6th Dragoon Guards (Carabiniers) and the Horse Artillery [USI; Rotton]. *Solid.*
2. **The Delhi regiments** were the 38th, 54th and 74th Native Infantry, plus a native field battery. The 38th were the most violent; many of the 74th helped their officers escape [MH p.53]. *Solid for the numbers; behaviour is per Mainodin.*
3. **The 85 troopers** were sentenced and shackled on parade on 9 May. Their refusal of cartridges dated from 24 April [USI; Luke]. *Solid.*
4. **Outbreak dates:** Meerut rose on Sunday 10 May (evening); Delhi was seized on Monday 11 May. *Solid.*
5. **Telegraph staff:** Charles Todd, "Assistant-in-Charge"; signallers William Brendish and J. W. Pilkington [memorial in Fanshawe p.331]. Pilkington had a withered leg and wore a special boot [Luke p.255]. Variant spellings: "Brandish", "Bendish", and "George" Brendish in family usage [IJAIS]. *Solid.*
6. **Telegraph office:** outside the walls, about one mile from the Kashmiri Gate and one mile from the Flagstaff Tower [Luke p.255]. *Single-source, but precise.*
7. **The 1902 memorial:** a granite obelisk unveiled 19 April 1902, about 800 yards from the Kashmiri Gate, in front of the then telegraph office (the old Dak Bungalow) [Fanshawe p.18]. By my inference it is probably *not* the 1857 office site.
8. **Fates of the telegraph staff:** Brendish retired on 1 September 1896. Pilkington "taken prisoner after doing so, but escaped. Died, Roorkee, 24th March 1867" [memorial]. *Solid.*
9. **The "telegraph saved India" line** is Robert Montgomery's. In 1857 he was Judicial Commissioner of the Punjab at Lahore [Luke p.252], not "Chief Commissioner of Oudh" (a later post). *Solid.*
10. **Bahadur Shah Zafar** was born 24 October 1775, so he was 81 in May 1857. His titles on the 1857 coins read "Suraj-u-din Bahadur Shah Ghazi" [JL pp.96-97; MH p.69]. His pen-name was Zafar. His favourite queen was Zinat Mahal and his chosen heir Mirza Jawan Bakht (Macdowell writes "Jumma Bukh") [Hodson p.345]. *Solid.*
11. **Mirza Mughal** (also Moghal, Mogul) was Commander-in-Chief until 2 July, then Adjutant-General [JL p.134; Sen p.84]. *Solid.*
12. **Muhammad Bakht Khan** was an artillery subahdar from Bareilly. He reached Delhi on 1-2 July and on 2 July received a shield, a sword and the title of General. He reported his force as "four regiments of foot, seven hundred cavalry, six horse artillery guns... fourteen elephants" with 4 lakhs of treasure [JL pp.133-35]. A spy letter spells him "Buktawa Khan" [KY p.335]. His authority collapsed in mid-to-late August [Sen p.101]. *Solid.*
13. **The rebel "Court":** a constitution (undated) in the Mutiny Papers sets up 10 members: 6 elected by the army (2 each from infantry, cavalry and artillery) and 4 civil. The president had a casting vote, and the King could overrule [Sen p.75, citing NAI Mutiny Papers Bundle 57, Nos. 539-41]. In late August Jiwan Lal records a "Court of twelve members, six to be appointed by the King and six by the Army" [JL p.205]. *Disputed composition: 10 or 12.*
14. **The Ridge landmarks:** Hindu Rao's House (held by the Sirmur Gurkhas under Maj. Reid), the Flagstaff Tower, the Observatory, an old Pathan mosque, Metcalfe House, Ludlow Castle, Sabzi Mandi and Kishanganj [Sen pp.79-80]. *Solid.*
15. **City gates in play:** Kashmiri, Mori, Kabul, Lahore, Ajmeri, Turkman, Delhi, Raj Ghat and Calcutta Gates. *Trap:* the Fort *also* has a Lahore Gate and a Delhi Gate. Mainodin lists troops at "Lahore Gate (Fort), Lahore Gate (City)" [MH p.61]. *Solid.*
16. **Size of the city:** the walled city was "seven miles in circuit" [Sen p.77]. *Solid.*
17. **British force:** about 6,600 by the end of June [Sen p.83]. Nicholson's column of 14 August was "two thousand four hundred infantry, six guns and some cavalry" [Sen p.98]. The effective strength before the assault "did not exceed nine thousand" [Sen p.99]. More than half the fighting men in September were Indian [Sen p.104]. *Solid for the order of magnitude.*
18. **Rebel force:** Mainodin's table for 12 May gives 5 infantry regiments (2,000), 1 cavalry regiment (350) and 1 battery (180), total 2,530 [MH p.61]. Mirza Mughal's later review counted "seventeen regiments of foot, twenty-two regiments of cavalry, in all about 9,000" [MH p.68]. Wilson estimated 40,000 before the assault; Hodson said 70,000 [Sen p.108]. *Disputed: the range is c. 2,500 (12 May) to 40,000+ (September).*
19. **Casualties:** British losses from 30 May to 20 September were 3,837 killed, wounded or missing. On assault day alone, 1,104 men and 66 officers [Sen pp.107-08]. *Solid as British official figures.*
20. **The 16 May killings:** the charge at Zafar's trial says "49 persons". A witness who asked the sweepers says 52, "only five or six" of them men [Trial c. pp.91-92]. Jiwan Lal says "nearly forty" were hidden in the Palace [JL p.93]. *Disputed: 40 to 52.*
21. **The powder factory explosion, 7 August:** Jiwan Lal says 494 dead with 13 escaped [JL p.186]. Mainodin says about 200 artificers [MH p.68]. *Disputed.*
22. **The Magazine:** nine defenders under Lt. George Willoughby; Conductor Scully fired the train. Victoria Crosses went to Forrest, Raynor and Buckley only (London Gazette, 18 June 1858). Willoughby was killed by villagers on the way to Meerut [Kaye II pp.89-90; Sen p.73]. *Solid. Note: Sen wrongly adds Shaw and Stewart as VC holders.*
23. **Money:** 1 rupee = 16 annas, 1 anna = 4 pice. A gold mohur was normally about 16 rupees, but sold at 32 in rebel Delhi on 21 May [JL p.100]. Sepoy pay: the Delhi regiments accepted Rs 7 a month, the cavalry demanded Rs 30 and were promised Rs 20 [JL p.105]. The King's perwanahs promised Rs 30 for infantry and Rs 50 for cavalry to regiments that came over [MH p.60]. The proclaimed exchange cap was no more than one anna discount per rupee [Sen p.102]. On the Ridge, bacon cost Rs 5 a pound (8 July) [Sen p.88] and beer Rs 15 a dozen [Sen p.97]. *Solid.*
24. **Units:** the kos is "about two miles" [KY glossary]. The tola is a weight; each chapati weighed "two tolahs" [MH p.39]. *Solid.*
25. **Scripts and languages:** the court wrote in Persian (rubakari, perwanah, shukka) and in Urdu in shikasta hand. The newspapers were the Urdu *Delhi Urdu Akhbar* (Maulvi Muhammad Baqir; renamed *Akhbar-uz-Zafar* from 12 July 1857) and the *Sadiq-ul-Akhbar* (translated at the trial as "Authentic News"). In English there was the *Delhi Gazette* [Trial; Safvi; Kaye II p.82]. *Solid.*
26. **Distances:** the 3rd Cavalry rode Meerut to Delhi overnight; the distance is usually given as 36-40 miles, though Kaye writes "thirty miles". Karnal is about 75 miles from Delhi. Humayun's Tomb is about 5-6 miles from the Palace: Hodson's party called it "the longest five miles", and Macdowell says six [Hodson pp.346, 349]. The Qutb is "about eleven miles south-west" [KY glossary]. *Approximate.*
27. **British officials:** Governor-General Lord Canning (not "Viceroy", a title used only from November 1858); Sir John Lawrence, Chief Commissioner of the Punjab; John Russell Colvin, Lieutenant-Governor of the North-Western Provinces at Agra. Commanders-in-Chief in turn: Anson (died 27 May), Barnard (died 5 July), Reed, then Archdale Wilson. *Solid.*

## 5. Real messages (seeds for "genuine")

1. **Brendish's message, 11 May, sent c. 2 pm** (as given in Anson's letter to Canning; Kaye II pp.137-38):
   > "We must leave office! All the bungalows are on fire—burning down by the Sepoys of Meerut. They came in this morning. We are off. Mr. C. Todd is dead, I think. He went out this morning, and has not yet returned. We learnt that nine Europeans are killed."

   Luke says Brendish remembered it ending "and now I'm off" [Luke p.260].
2. **Brigadier Graves's official message, sent by Pilkington c. 3 pm** [Kaye II p.138; Luke p.262]:
   > "Cantonments in a state of siege. Mutineers from Meerut—Third Light Cavalry—numbers not known—said to be a hundred and fifty men. Cut off communication with Meerut. Taken possession of the Bridge of Boats. Fifty-fourth Native Infantry sent against them, but would not act. Several officers killed and wounded. City in a state of considerable excitement. Troops sent down, but nothing known yet. Information will be forwarded."
3. **The Meerut postmaster to Agra, midnight 10 May** [USI]:
   > "Cavalry have risen setting fire to houses having killed or wounded all Europeans they could find. If aunt intends starting tomorrow please detain her."
4. **Zafar to Mirza Mughal, undated, c. 21 May ("ten days have now elapsed")** [Trial c. pp.131-32]:
   > "Our Son, the Illustrious and Valiant... Repeated injunctions have been issued prohibiting plunder and aggression in the city, but all to no purpose."

   He threatens to "assum[e] the garb of a religious mendicant, to proceed first and stay at the shrine of the saint Khwaja Sahib... to go eventually to Mecca." The same letter admits the troops asked "that all the (European) prisoners should be killed at once. This was also complied with." See section 10.
5. **Court Diary, 16 May** [Trial c. p.92]:
   > "49 English were prisoners and the army demanded that they should be given over to them for slaughter. The King delivered them up, saying 'The army may do as they please.'"
6. **Zafar to Mirza Mughal and Mirza Khizr Sultan, 27 June** [Sen p.85, from the Mutiny Papers]:
   > "Not a day has elapsed, since the arrival of the Army, and its taking up quarters in the City, that petitions from the towns-people have not been submitted, representing the excesses committed by numerous Infantry Sepoys."
7. **A petition from Syed Abdullah, 29 June** [Sen p.86]:
   > "The whole of the Autumnal crop... of the Fasli year 1265, has been totally devastated... the very implements of Agriculture, such as ploughs, the wood-work on wells, have been all carried away."
8. **Drum proclamations recorded by Jiwan Lal:**
   - 21 May: "Hindus and Mahommedans must not quarrel" [JL p.100].
   - 2 July, Bakht Khan's order: "all shopkeepers were to keep arms... Any soldier caught plundering was to have his arm severed from his body" [JL p.135].
9. **Zafar's order on cow-killing, 28 July** [JL p.170]:
   > "no cows were to be killed within the city during the festival of the Eed, and if any Mahommedan should do so he would be blown away from a gun."
10. **Zafar's verse sent to Bakht Khan, 2 August** (Jiwan Lal's version, in Metcalfe's translation) [JL p.177]:
    > "Celebrate the festival of the Eed Kurban by great slaughter; Put our enemies to the edge of the sword—spare not!"
11. **The commander-in-chief's sealed order to the Kotwal, 13 September** [Trial c. p.131]:
    > "Intelligence has just been brought that the English intend making a general attack to-night... proclaimed by beat of drum throughout the city, that it is incumbent on all the inhabitants whether Hindu or Mahomedan... to assemble directly in the direction of the Cashmere Gate, bringing iron picks and shovels."
12. **Delhi Urdu Akhbar, No. XXXIV, 23 August** [Trial c. p.130]:
    > "Some people coming from the direction of Peshawar and the Panjab, state that the Persian army has reached Attok. Although I do not fully believe this myself, I have published it as hearsay gossip."
13. **Sadiq-ul-Akhbar, 23 March**, on a forged "Persian" placard stuck on the back wall of the Jama Masjid. It mocks the forger, "Muhammad Sadik Khan", who it says deserves "a double-soled shoe steeped in vinegar" on his "scabby cranium" [Trial c. p.128].
14. **A spy's letter, 24-25 August** [KY p.335]:
    > "General Buktawa Khan moved on to Alleepore, via Nujufghurh, with six Regiments of Infantry, two of Cavalry, and twelve guns... A document to Mirza Ilahi Baksh and Zeenut Mahal Begum will even now procure the desired aid from the garrison."
15. **Rao Tula Ram of Rewari to a noble, read at the darbar on 31 July** [JL p.174]:
    > "Are you intoxicated that you think the English are going away from Hindustan? They will most assuredly return and will destroy you."

## 6. Real deception

- **A planted letter to frame ministers.** In May, "some enemy" wrote a letter in the names of Mahbub Ali Khan and Hakim Ahsanullah Khan to the Lieutenant-Governor at Agra and let it fall into the gate guards' hands. Zafar "saw through the treachery" [MH p.59]. In August a compromising letter was found in Ahsanullah's house; Cooper says Rajab Ali planted it on purpose [Sen p.94].
- **A forged petition from Jammu.** A petition "believed to have been forged", supposedly from Raja Gulab Singh, promised to march on Delhi and punish Patiala on the way [Ahsanullah's statement, Majumdar p.126].
- **A fake Persian proclamation (March 1857).** It was pasted on the Jama Masjid and claimed 500 Persian soldiers were already in Delhi in disguise. The rebel press then ran Persian-army rumours all summer: Kandahar, then 50,000 men, then Attock, then the Bolan [Trial pp.128-31].
- **A disguised regiment at Badli-ki-Serai, 8 June.** Ahmed Khan, a Resaldar of the 4th Irregular Cavalry, promised the King that his men would defect. Rebels cheered "Deen Deen!" at cavalry in that uniform, which wheeled away to unmask a battery [MH pp.62-63]. *Single-source.*
- **Sabotage of guns:** guns spiked or filled with kankar and string (26-27 May) [JL pp.103-04]. On the British side, gun lascars put "little stones" in the priming powder (16 August) and two were hanged [Sen p.100].
- **Hodson's reward offer.** Hodson promised spies 1,000 rupees to blow up the powder factory. "The reward was never claimed, nor did the men ever return" [Metcalfe's footnote, MH p.68]. Sen says the cause of the 7 August explosion is unknown [Sen p.94].
- **Hiding letters:** India paper inside fake tape, a hollow stick, a shoe sole [KY pp.243-44]. Sherer wrote: "You must take a cipher off all these numbers, except the sick" (troop numbers inflated tenfold in case the letter was caught) [KY p.236]. Eden: "I have written to him twice lately in French" [KY p.189].
- **Spy reports that flatter their readers.** Norman says the city letters were "coloured in a high degree to please us" [KY p.173].
- **False alarms and false identities:**
  - the Eid procession taken for a British advance (25 May);
  - a European in fortune-teller's dress accused of being John Lawrence (29 May);
  - Gwalior men suspected of being a British advance guard in disguise (28 May) [JL pp.102-07].
- **Codenames and disguise.** Metcalfe became "Shere Khan", dressed "as a native soldier" [MH p.57]. Jiwan Lal paid "two Brahmins... and two jats" to watch the gates and the Palace [JL p.77].
- **Royal back-channels.** Zinat Mahal's emissary went to Greathed on 21 August. Princes offered "to destroy the Bridge" in return for favour [Sen pp.95-96].
- **Bait at Humayun's Tomb.** Hodson spread reports of an advance on the Qutb to push the King into surrender [Hodson p.344].

## 7. Tempting wrong variants (DESIGN INPUT: these are deliberately false)

- **Names:** "Charles Tod"; "William Brandish"; "J. W. Pilkinton"; "Sir Thomas Metcalfe" on 11 May (he died in 1853; his son Theophilus was there); "Mirza Moghal Beg"; "Bakht Singh"; "Hakim Ehsan Ali"; "Rajab Khan"; "Zeenat Begum of Lucknow".
- **Regiments, off by one:**
  - "3rd Native Infantry" or "4th Light Cavalry" at Meerut;
  - "12th and 21st NI";
  - "34th NI at Delhi" (the 34th is the Barrackpore regiment);
  - "37th / 57th / 74th".
- **Places:**
  - **Gates:** "Kashmiri Gate" for the Raj Ghat entry; "Ajmeri Gate" for the princes' shooting (it was near the Delhi Gate). The Fort's Lahore Gate and the city's Lahore Gate are not the same.
  - **Ridge landmarks:** "Hindu Rai's house"; "Flagstaff Bastion" instead of the Flagstaff Tower.
  - **Others:** "Humayun's Tomb at Mehrauli"; "Jhajjar" as Metcalfe's first refuge (he first hid at Bhura Khan Mewati's house, then went to Jhajjar).
- **Times and dates:**
  - "office closed 10 to 5 on Sundays";
  - a Brendish message sent "at 9 am";
  - 11 May called a "Sunday" (it was Monday);
  - Eid on 24 May (Jiwan Lal has the 25th);
  - Zafar surrendering on 14 September;
  - Anson dying "at Ambala".
- **Anachronisms:**
  - "Viceroy Canning" (the title began November 1858);
  - "Empress" on coins (1877);
  - the Clock Tower in Chandni Chowk (built later);
  - a railway to Delhi (not until the 1860s);
  - "New Delhi";
  - "Lee-Enfield" (the rifle in question is the 1853 Enfield);
  - "Indian Army" (the force was the Bengal Army);
  - "Lt-Governor of the Punjab" (Lawrence was Chief Commissioner).
- **Habits that give away a forger:**
  - **On a Delhi court paper:** an English-style signature instead of a seal; a date like "11th May" with no Hijri date; no invocation at the head; no honorifics ("May you be protected"; "Our Son, the Illustrious and Valiant").
  - **On a British field telegram:** flowery Persianate praise.
  - **On a cossid letter:** thick paper.
  - **On the telegraph:** the sender ignores "What is your name?".
  - **In a darbar scene:** soldiers in "caps instead of turbans" and shoes on the carpet are real complaints [Trial c. p.109, c. p.131]. A forger who has Zafar *praise* such conduct is out of character.

## 8. Myths and the record

1. **"The signaller died with his hand on the key."** False. Cooper's 1862 guide and Cave-Browne repeated it, but Fanshawe notes Brendish was "still alive" in 1902 [Fanshawe p.108; Luke p.252].
2. **"The Sepoys have come in from Meerut and are burning everything... We must shut up."** This is the dramatised popular version [Luke p.252]. Even Sen quotes it [Sen p.74]. Use Kaye's text in section 5 instead.
3. **"Brendish won the VC."** Family lore only [IJAIS]. The Delhi VCs went to the Magazine officers.
4. **"Fraser slept with the warning letter in his pocket."** Mainodin reports it as what is "said" "among the natives" [MH p.42]. Jiwan Lal's version differs [JL p.78n]. *Unverified.*
5. **"The chapatis were a coded call to revolt."**
   - **What the record shows:** they circulated from late January (Thornhill at Mathura) and through February; each was five cakes of "barley and wheat flour... the size of the palm" [MH p.39].
   - **What carriers thought:** Metcalfe told the trial carriers "believed it to be done by order of the English Government" and that the meaning "was not understood" [Trial c. p.83].
   - **What historians say:** Majumdar: "more than a dozen interpretations" [Majumdar p.203]. Wagner argues the cakes were a blank onto which people projected fears.
6. **"A national plot fixed the rising for 31 May"; "red lotus flowers passed from regiment to regiment."** Both rest on single British reports that Savarkar used [Savarkar]. Majumdar calls Malleson's "Executive Council" unsupported [Majumdar pp.181-82]. Sen's index reads "no pre-planning" [Sen pp.398-405].
7. **"Delhi was besieged."** Sen: "neither the city of Delhi, nor the hostile camp on the ridge was ever besieged". The Mathura road stayed open [Sen p.79].
8. **"Zafar was the revolt's planner" vs "Zafar was a mere puppet."** Both flatten him.
   - Azad's foreword says the uprising "was as much a surprise to Bahadur Shah as to the British" [Sen p.ix].
   - He also governed: he banned cow-killing, disgraced Abu Bakr, and wrote orders and verse.
   - The 16 May diary entry implicates him.
9. **"The princes were shot at the Diwan-i-Am" (Mainodin, MH p.71).** Wrong. Hodson's own account puts it on the road near the city [Hodson p.341].
10. **"Lagta nahin hai dil mera / do gaz zameen" was written by Zafar in Rangoon.** The attribution is disputed. Some credit Muztar Khairabadi; the link to Zafar was popularised by later recordings. Do not quote it as Zafar's.
11. **The "Plassey centenary prophecy" on 23 June.** British writers credited it; Sen notes it is strange the sepoys would keep the English calendar [Sen p.82n]. The Delhi press actually cited a 100-year prophecy of Shah Ni'matullah [Trial c. p.130].
12. **"Maulvi Baqir: martyr journalist" vs "Baqir: British informer."** Dalrymple suggests he turned informer; Moosvi and Safvi find no archival proof [Safvi]. *Disputed.*

## 9. Sensory world

- **Telegraph.** Signals were read from **needle movements**: the Ambala staff saw "movements on the needle" [Luke p.265]. A rattling Morse sounder is an anachronism risk. Use needle ticks against stops, plus a call bell. There was a cable house on each bank of the Jumna; the office was a lone building on the road with *Delhi Gazette* messengers waiting outside [Luke pp.255-58].
- **The city:**
  - a 21-gun salute at night [JL p.82];
  - the Magazine blast, "a sound like roll of thunder, with an earthquake" [JL p.81];
  - proclamations "by beat of drum";
  - green-clad men on camels with red turbans crying "the drum of religion has sounded" [MH p.48];
  - cries of "Allah-dad!" at the attack on Douglas's rooms [JL p.81] and of "Din!" [Sen p.293];
  - the azan and temple bells;
  - the palace naqqar khana (music gallery), beside which the 16 May killings took place [Trial];
  - cannon fire "night and day" from June [MH p.64]: "the whole city had become accustomed to the sound of cannon" [MH p.69].
- **Weather.** May heat, with khus tatties and the punkah [USI]. A wind storm on 30 June sank the bridge of boats [JL p.132]. August rains flooded the country [JL p.177]. Cholera killed both Anson and Barnard.
- **A munshi's desk:**
  - reed pens (qalam) and ink;
  - Persian court orders with the royal seal; petitions in shikasta hand;
  - thin, tough "India paper" for secret notes [KY p.335];
  - a palki waiting at the door [JL p.75];
  - a box of pan;
  - kewra water from Jaipur presented at court [JL p.95];
  - nazars of gold mohurs and rupees.
- **Court objects:**
  - the chobdar's mace;
  - the Red Purdah (Lal Parda) gate of the palace [MH p.49];
  - the Diwan-i-Khas, which no one entered on horseback;
  - peacock-feather fans behind the captive King, who wore white with "a conical-shaped turban" [Griffiths in Sen p.111].
- **Food and money:**
  - five rupees given "to purchase sugar" for sherbet [MH p.49];
  - "a dish of pillau" promised to the frightened King [MH p.71];
  - sweets sent to welcome arriving sappers [JL p.96];
  - bacon and Parsi traders' beer on the Ridge.
- **Clothes and people:**
  - Metcalfe fleeing "without any clothes save his shirt and underdrawers" [MH p.43];
  - a woman jihadi in a green turban captured by the British [Greathed in Sen p.92n];
  - Gujar raiders stripping scouts naked;
  - bullock-carts (ruths) at Humayun's Tomb [Hodson p.340].

## 10. Sensitivity notes and the range of views

- **The name of the event is itself a debate, so show it.**
  - **British imperial historians:** "Sepoy Mutiny" or conspiracy (Kaye, Malleson).
  - **Savarkar (1909):** a planned "War of Independence".
  - **Marx and Engels:** contemporary 1857-58 articles, later compiled as *The First Indian War of Independence*.
  - **Sen (1957, Government of India):** "What began as a fight for religion ended as a war of independence" [Sen p.411]. Azad's foreword calls early nationalist accounts "not history but mere political propaganda" [Sen p.ix].
  - **Majumdar (1957):** "To regard the outbreak of 1857 as either national in character or a war for independence... betrays a lack of true knowledge" [Majumdar p.237]. He also documented British atrocities from British records [p.xii].
  - **Later work:** Stokes and the subaltern historians stress peasant and local motives. Mukherjee writes on Awadh and on violence. Dalrymple centres Delhi and its religious tensions. Farooqui uses ordinary people's petitions. Wagner focuses on rumour and panic.
- **Atrocities on both sides must sit side by side.**
  - **Rebel side:** the 16 May killings of women and children.
  - **British side:** British soldiers killing loyal camp-followers on 9 July [Kaye in Sen p.89]; the sack of the city and summary hangings; "the guiltless shared the same fate as the guilty" [MH p.72]. Ghalib's brother was killed in the street [MH p.72].
  - Neither side should be shown as a sermon.
- **Communal framing.** The record shows *both* jihadi pressure and its explicit rejection by the King and by many soldiers (sections 5 and 8). Mainodin's narrative opens with a Hindu-Muslim mosque dispute at Hanumangarhi. Metcalfe's 1898 introduction generalises about "Mahommedans" and "Hindus". Treat both as period bias and do not reproduce them as fact.
- **Zafar's role.** Present the evidence on both sides: the 16 May diary entry and his own letter ("This was also complied with") against the coercion visible everywhere ("I have no force to obey my orders") [MH p.49]. Sen judges him "a victim of circumstances" [Sen p.110].
- **Suggested on-screen line:** "Historians still argue whether this was a mutiny, a revolt, or a war of independence. The letters you stamp are real, or close to it. Judge the facts."

## 12. Who sided with the British, with receipts

The tone brief is noted. Below are documented facts and quotable lines, with complications kept, because the creator's credibility depends on them.

### Princely states and chiefs

- **Patiala (Maharaja Narinder Singh).**
  - Cave-Browne: "The Puttiala chief received the letter that night, and needed no second call: within eighteen hours of its being despatched he was encamped at Taysomlee, some eight miles from Umballa" [CB I p.190].
  - He guarded the Grand Trunk Road with "a network of levies and police, altogether numbering about 5000 men" [CB I p.221].
  - **Ignored Zafar's appeal:** Zafar's shukka (royal letter) to Patiala got no reply [Ahsanullah, in Majumdar p.126].
  - **Reward:** "Perghanah Narnal, which yielded a revenue of 200,000 rupees, was given to Puttiala" [MH p.73]. Narnaul had belonged to Jhajjar, whose Nawab was hanged.
- **Jind (Raja Sarup Singh).**
  - "The Jheend rajah had been even more prompt" [CB I p.190].
  - "the gallant Rajah of Jheend, with his little well-organised force of 800 men of all arms... pressing on as the vanguard of the army" [CB I p.221].
  - **Reward:** Dadri "was confiscated and made over to the Maharajah of Jeend" [MH p.74].
- **Nabha.** "Perghanah Kauli was given to the Rajah of Nabha" [MH p.74]. Standard accounts name other ex-Jhajjar tracts; *I have not verified these.* The 1860 Ambala darbar exempted Patiala, Jind and Nabha from the doctrine of lapse (secondary sources only).
- **Kapurthala (Raja Randhir Singh).** "the Raja of Kapurthala detailed his troops for the protection of Jullundur" [Sen p.329]. His later Oudh service and estates are *not verified here.*
- **Gwalior (Maharaja Jayajirao Scindia; Dewan Dinkar Rao).**
  - **Keeping the Contingent idle:** Sen says Scindia "pretended to take [the mutinied Contingent] into his service... By various devices he kept them quiescent till September, 1857... Sindhia had rendered a great service to his friends" [Sen p.291]. "The English friends of His Highness gave him and his Chief Minister Sir Dinkar Rao Rajwade credit for keeping the insurgents inactive" [Sen p.231].
  - **Seen from rebel Delhi:** "The Jodhpur and Gwalior Chiefs would appear to be determined to remain staunch... The mutiny of their troops did not alienate those Chiefs personally from the British" [Ahsanullah, in Majumdar p.126].
  - **The 21-gun salute:** "The Sindhia fired a salute of twenty-one guns on the fall of Jhansi" [Majumdar p.238].
  - **Canning's fear:** Majumdar reports Canning saying "If Sindhia joins the rebels I will pack off tomorrow" [Majumdar p.278].
  - **Where the popular version flattens:**
    - The Gwalior Contingent itself rose on the night of 14 June 1857 [Sen p.291] and later fought under Tatya Tope.
    - On 1 June 1858 Scindia's own army "fraternised" with the rebels with a cry of "Din". Scindia "rode to Dholpur on his way to Agra" [Sen pp.292-93].
    - Rebel opinion called Scindia and Dinkar Rao "nothing but Christians" [Sen p.292].
    - Subhadra Kumari Chauhan's line "अंग्रेज़ों के मित्र सिंधिया ने छोड़ी राजधानी थी" ("Scindia, friend of the English, abandoned his capital") matches the record of his flight. It omits that his soldiers and the Contingent fought *against* the British.
- **Hyderabad (Nizam Afzal-ud-Daula; minister Salar Jung).**
  - Majumdar quotes a contemporary Englishman: "Nothing but the [influence] of Salar Jung could prevent an outbreak in Hyderabad" [Majumdar p.278]. The OCR of one word is garbled; check the printed page.
  - On 17 July 1857 a Rohilla attack on the Residency led by Turrebaz Khan was repulsed (secondary sources).
  - Irony: Zafar had sent a khilat (robe of honour) and titles to the new Nizam [MH p.64].
- **Nepal (Jung Bahadur Rana).**
  - Sen: he "wanted to cement his amity with the British by proving himself a friend in need".
  - Forces: 3,000 Gurkhas went in from July 1857. He himself crossed on 21 December "with an army of 10,000 men" (Majumdar says 9,000) and reached Lucknow on 11 March 1858 [Sen pp.237-38; Majumdar p.76].
  - Canning on the delay to wait for him: "It would drive him wild to find himself jockeyed out of all share in the great campaign" [Sen p.238].
  - **Reward:** the western Terai (the "Naya Muluk") was returned by the treaty of 1 November 1860, and he was made GCB (secondary sources).
- **Others who hedged and were hanged anyway.** The Raja of Ballabhgarh and the Nawab of Jhajjar paid homage to Zafar while writing to the British; both were hanged [Sen p.111; KY p.113]. Loyalty to neither side saved them.

### Informers and spies inside Delhi

- **Mirza Ilahi Bakhsh.**
  - Dalrymple: father-in-law of Mirza Fakhru and grandfather of Mirza Abu Bakr; "in close contact with William Hodson throughout the siege". He was decisive in getting Zafar to surrender, then identified relatives who had sympathised with the rebels. Having bought his own safety "at the cost of most of his family, including his own grandson", he became known as the "Traitor of Delhi" [Dalrymple, Dramatis Personae, Spanish edition p. xix-xx].
  - Sen: "Mirza Ilahi Baksh was busy working in the interest of the English" [Sen p.95]. Zafar yielded "to the temptation of life held out by Rajab Ali and Ilahi Baksh" [Sen p.113].
  - Hodson's emissary was "one of the inferior scions of the royal family (purchased for the purpose by the promise of his life)" [Hodson p.340].
  - Complication: he twice rescued Jiwan Lal from extortion [JL pp.177, 188-90].
- **Maulvi Rajab Ali.**
  - Hodson: "The Intelligence Department is mine exclusively, and I have for this line Sir Henry's old friend, the one-eyed Moulvie, Rujub Alee, so I shall get the best news in the country" [Hodson p.~25].
  - "busy not only in espionage but in sowing dissension in the enemy ranks" [Sen p.83].
  - Alleged to have planted the letter against Ahsanullah [Sen p.94]. He left the city after the 7 August spy-hunt [Sen p.95n].
  - He announced the royal ladies at the surrender [Hodson p.345].
  - His later reward: *not verified.*
- **Munshi Jiwan Lal.**
  - In his own words: "for many years I had eaten the salt of the English Government and wished it well" [JL p.77]. He paid four informants [JL pp.77-78].
  - Metcalfe: "No more trustworthy or loyal native servant has the British Government ever had".
  - **Reward:** made an honorary Magistrate and Municipal Commissioner [MH Intro pp.2-3].
- **Gauri Shankar Sukul.** Dalrymple's dramatis personae: commander of the Haryana regiment, "the most prominent British spy and agent provocateur" inside rebel ranks. *Single secondary source; no primary receipt found.*
- **Hakim Ahsanullah Khan.** Suspected throughout; Zafar hid him on 7 August. Dalrymple says he gave evidence against his master at the trial in return for a pardon. His statements are in the Trial record (13th day, 11 February 1858).
- **Complications, not collaborators.** Mainodin both saved Metcalfe's life and commanded a rebel regiment; he was tried, acquitted, and his family later received a grant [MH pp.4, 28-31]. Saving lives (Mainodin, Bhura Khan Mewati, Greathed's servant Golab Khan at Meerut [USI]) is not the same as spying.

### Rewarded afterwards (receipts)
- Patiala received Narnaul, plus Kanaud by purchase, "on account of his steadfast loyalty" [MH p.73]. Jind received Dadri; Nabha received "Kauli" [MH p.74].
- Brendish received a special pension equal to full pay [Fanshawe p.108].
- Jiwan Lal received honorary magistracy [MH p.3].
- Hodson chose swords bearing "the name of 'Nadir Shah'" and "the seal of Jehan Gire" from the King's arms, meaning to present them to the Queen [Hodson p.346].
- Sir Syed Ahmad Khan "was a staunch supporter of the British during the Mutiny and yet rose to be the undisputed leader of Muslims in U.P." [Majumdar p.238]. He also wrote a critique of British policy, *Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind*, which Lees translated in 1860 [Majumdar p.xv].

### Where the "traitors" story flattens
- Fighting for the British was broad and was not one community. More than half the fighting men on the Ridge in September were Indian [Sen p.104]: Gurkhas, Punjabi Muslims, Pathans, Sikhs, and Hindustanis who "remained singularly faithful" [Gough in Sen p.100].
- Punjabis and Sikhs also served on the rebel side. A spy wrote from inside Delhi: "The Sikhs have been scattered among the different regiments" [KY p.335]. Two "regiments of Punjabis" petitioned Zafar on 27 May [JL p.103].
- Many Delhi bankers, of every faith, paid both sides under duress [Sen pp.101-02].
- **Advice for the game:** joke about named people and their documented acts, never about communities.
